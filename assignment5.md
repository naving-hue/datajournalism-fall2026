## Lede

The story used an anecdotal lede about the Petersen family. The first paragraph was arresting — why is she paying acquaintances to take her kid to school? The second answers that question — because they lost their apartment. But then that leaves me wondering, OK, is this just a story about a homeless family? But the third and fourth say what this is really going to be about: the fact that there’s a program that entitled them to free school bus transportation, and it’s not being communicated to the people who need it.

Using an anecdotal lede like this helps humanize the issue the story is about, so I do think it’s effective. It takes a little longer than I would have liked to get to the payoff. Perhaps the sentence about having to switch districts could have been cut or moved down lower in the story. 

## The data

The analysis used linear regression to compare the number of identified homeless students with other metrics that typically correlate to homelessness: free lunch programs and poverty rates. It makes an assumption that in places where there are more use of free and reduced lunch programs and higher poverty rates, you would expect more students to be homeless. The story then backs this assumption up with data and quotes from experts. It looks like there was a map in the story that isn’t working now, but I would have been interested to be able to explore these rates on my own for places I’m interested in.

## Numbers

For a story based on data, it kept pacing well throughout and did not inundate the reader with numbers. 

When it did have more number-heavy sections, it made use of data visualizations to offload some of the work from the text. In this case, for example, it made a trend statement without any numbers, then immediately used a data visualization to give the full data:

The academic cost is not equally shared. Black and Latino children experience homelessness at disproportionate rates, Public Integrity’s analysis showed. Nationally, American Indian or Alaska Native students were also over-represented, as were students with disabilities.

 It also kept the numbers simple and easy to understand, for example:

DeSoto County, Mississippi, for instance, identified fewer than 300 homeless students, according to state records Public Integrity reviewed. Its share of students eligible for free- or reduced-price lunches suggests the district has three times the number it reported.

That’s not the only reason to suspect an undercount. In 2018, local landlords filed more than 4,000 eviction cases, according to an estimate from Princeton University’s Eviction Lab.

## Data viz:
[insert etc here]
The data visualizations in this story could have been more effective. 

For example, there was a fairly large map in the last section just showing where Temecula is in Southern California — information that isn’t necessarily important to the story. It would have been different if the map showed where the Petersen family moved, or if the proximity between Temecula and their new address was shown. 

There was also a table with the per-capita funding per homeless student. But without a baseline of how much is “enough,” the numbers were not meaningful on their own. (That baseline is included in the story, but not in the graphic itself).

I did like the use of the race-based graphic to offload some of the data from the story to a chart, as mentioned above. However, it was not explicitly clear what the percentages represented. I’m assuming that it is the percentage of students in each category (enrolled and homeless) who fall within each demographic. However, it could also be read as the percentage of students in that demographic who are enrolled or homeless. This is almost certainly not the case, but the chart labeling does not make that clear.

I thought the second section of the story, which dealt with race, was less revelatory, than the lower sections. The main finding of that second section appeared to be that homelessness is a larger issue for Black, Latino and disabled students. This wasn’t surprising to me as a reader or to the sources interviewed.
